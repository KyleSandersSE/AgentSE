## What worked

- **"So you guys can't even touch your own system?"** — the question that opened the whole account. Simple, direct, said their situation back to them as a problem they hadn't fully named.
- **The 2 AM scenario** — *"a pump station goes down at 2 AM. Call Telstar and wait, or go out with a laptop and bring it back up?"* Concrete, operational, emotionally real. Best value articulation across all three calls. Reuse verbatim.
- **Future-pacing the pain** — *"With all the new PLCs, can you imagine this becoming a bigger problem? And if you get SCADA site-wide, it could be a really big problem."* Took a one-station annoyance and scaled it into a citywide risk. This is the single most advanced selling move in any of the three transcripts.
- **Preempting the Telstar landmine** — *"That doesn't mean cutting them out by any means."* The customer immediately relaxed and validated the relationship. In a public agency with an incumbent, that sentence is the difference between a conversation and a wall.
- **Got the energy number.** $15k/month, ~20% of departmental budget. Asked, then asked again for the percentage. Direct fix of the California Dairies miss.
- **Anchored high on cost** — *"20, 30, tens of thousands?"* They pushed back to ~$2,500/visit, which is exactly what anchoring is for.
- **Full org chart obtained** — *"Could you paint the picture, the tree of people?"* Estefan → Diego → Supervisor → Zeke/Anthony, plus who is technically savvy (Diego).
- **Fiscal mechanics** — July–July, this year tight, next year set, next talks "toward the end of the early year," and *"where can we be on the docket?"* That is procurement-aware selling.
- **Territory presence** — knew Telstar's people (Connor, Corey, Frank, Steve), Medallion's people (Erin, Chad), remembered the Chick-fil-A visit and Fernando. This is a year of relationship compounding.

## The structural problem: Telstar

Telstar is the incumbent integrator, **holds the programming license that creates the pain being sold against**, is mid-delivery on the citywide upgrade, and is one of Schneider's own close local partners.

Selling EAE here means selling against a channel partner's recurring service revenue while likely needing that partner to deliver the work.

Third-party validation already exists and is strong — the electrical instructor told the customer, unprompted: *"some companies get the permit, that's where they make the money, every time you call them that's where you spend the money."* And: *"I can make my own program and charge the city just for programming."* The customer repeated this back as their own grievance.

**Decide the Telstar posture before the Diego meeting, not during it.** The constructive frame: EAE lets Telstar bid and win more work because they stop being the bottleneck on every truck roll, and end-user training becomes something Telstar offers rather than something that threatens them. If Telstar won't play, know that before walking into Diego's office.

## The wedge: the other 30 stations

Telstar's project covers 12 of ~42 lift stations. The remaining ~30 have no PLC, no SCADA, no visibility — and the customer said plainly: *"Ideally, we want SCADA for everything."*

That is not competing with Telstar's current scope. It is the next project, and it is a fit for the Altivar dPAC play Kyle already raised — one box instead of PLC + drive + SCADA node, which is exactly what a budget-constrained small utility needs. The pitch writes itself: *you are about to have two classes of lift station, and the 30 you cannot see are where the overflows happen.*

# Closing segment — additional findings

## The pump standardization story — biggest miss of the call

Jeremy walked through it unprompted:

- Flygt pumps installed ~2004–2007 reached end of life
- Replacements went **obsolete**; Flygt "weren't responding fast enough"
- Under time pressure (station needs 3 of 4 pumps during high flow) they bought **Homa** off the shelf because it matched the curve
- The pump fit — but the **guide rails and base elbows were Flygt-proprietary**, so it needed an adapter
- Fleet is now one Flygt, one Homa, one Flygt, one Crane
- Telstar's recommendation: **standardize on Homa so the base elbow accepts any pump.** "It's not gonna be proprietary to just Flygt."

That is the EAE thesis, told back to him in hardware form, by the customer, complete with the lesson already learned and the decision already made — *on pumps*.

**The line that was available and not used:**

> "You just told me why you're standardizing on Homa — so the base elbow fits whatever pump you can actually get. That's exactly what we do with controls. Right now your PLC is a Flygt guide rail: one vendor, one programmer, one phone number. We make it a Homa base elbow."

A customer-supplied analogy, already emotionally validated, requiring no explanation. **This is the single highest-value thing missed across all three calls.**

## MicroLogix — verify immediately

Hardware named: VFDs likely **Schneider Altivar** (Telstar's spec), PLC believed to be **Allen-Bradley MicroLogix**.

If that is correct, Telstar is installing *new* PLCs on a Rockwell platform that has already been announced end-of-life. **Verify before the October meeting.** If it holds, it is the most legitimate and least aggressive argument available: the city is spending capital today on a controller with a published sunset, at 12 of 42 sites, with 30 more to come.

The Altivar detail also matters in the other direction — Schneider drives are already going in, which makes the Altivar dPAC (drive-as-controller) play materially easier at the remaining 30 stations.
